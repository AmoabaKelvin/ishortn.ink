A flyer gets a few seconds of attention. Someone walks past a noticeboard, picks one up at a counter or finds it under the door, and either acts on it right then or forgets it. A QR code can turn those seconds into a visit to your booking form, menu or event page. That only happens if it scans on the first try and opens a page worth landing on.

This guide covers the decisions that make a flyer code work: what it should link to, whether you need a static or dynamic code, how big to print it, where to put it, and how to tell which batch of flyers brought people in.

We make iShortn, a link shortener with free dynamic QR codes. Most of the advice works with any QR tool, and we'll say where you don't need ours.

## The short version

1. Link the code to a page that matches the flyer's offer, not your homepage.
2. Use a static code if that page will never move. Use a dynamic one if details might change or you want scan counts.
3. Print it at least 2.5 cm (1 inch) wide on a handout, and bigger the further away people will stand.
4. Put it next to the call to action, with a line of text that says what scanning does.
5. Print one test copy and scan it with two different phones before you order the batch.

## Step 1: Decide where the code should go

Send people to the page that finishes the job the flyer started. A flyer for a yoga class should open the booking page for that class. A flyer for a bake sale should open the event details or the pre-order form. Your homepage makes people hunt, and on a phone most of them won't.

Before you go further, open that page on your phone over mobile data and check three things:

- It loads in a few seconds.
- You can read it without pinching and zooming.
- The main action (book, sign up, order, RSVP) is visible without much scrolling.

If you want people to pay you, follow you or message you, the apps already make codes for that. Venmo, Cash App, Instagram and WhatsApp all have their own QR codes, and it's fine to print one of those. You just can't change where they point or count the scans, so for a flyer you'll hand out in the hundreds, a code you control is usually the better choice.

## Step 2: Static or dynamic?

**A static code** has your link built into the pattern. It's free, needs no account and never expires. But you can't change it after printing, and it can't count scans.

**A dynamic code** contains a short link that forwards to your page. You can change where it goes at any time, and every scan gets counted.

Flyers are the classic case for dynamic codes, for three reasons:

- **Details change after printing.** The venue moves, the date slips, the sign-up form gets replaced. With a dynamic code you update the link and the flyers already on noticeboards keep working.
- **Flyers outlive the event.** People find old flyers for weeks. After the event you can point the code at photos, a recap or the next date, instead of a dead page.
- **You'll want to know if it worked.** Printing costs money. Scan counts tell you whether it was worth it.

One warning: some QR generators switch dynamic codes off when a free trial ends, and every flyer you printed stops working. Check the rules before you print. We cover this in [why QR codes stop working](/blog/qr-code-stopped-working).

A static code is fine when the link will never change, for example a page on your own site at a permanent address, and you don't care about scan counts. Our [free QR code guide](/blog/free-qr-code-generator-guide) covers the difference between the two in more detail.

## Step 4: Add it to your flyer

Whatever you design in, the process is the same: upload the PNG and place it as an image.

- **Canva:** upload the PNG under **Uploads**, then drag it onto the design. Canva also makes its own QR codes. According to [Canva's help page](https://www.canva.com/help/create-and-manage-qr-codes/), its static codes can't be changed after creation and don't track scans. Its dynamic codes can be edited, but scan insights need a Business, Enterprise or Nonprofit plan, and dynamic codes on the Free and Pro plans carry the Canva logo. If you want an editable code with scan counts and no logo on a free plan, make it elsewhere and upload it.
- **Word or Google Docs:** use **Insert > Pictures** (Word) or **Insert > Image** (Docs). Set text wrapping so the code doesn't shift when you edit the text around it.
- **A print shop:** send the PNG with your design files and tell them not to resize it unevenly.

Three rules while you place it:

- **Keep it square.** Drag a corner, never a side. A stretched code often won't scan.
- **Leave a clear border.** Every QR code needs an empty margin around it, called the quiet zone. [Denso Wave](https://www.qrcode.com/en/howto/code.html), which invented the QR code, specifies a margin four modules wide (a module is one of the small squares). Don't let text, a photo or a coloured background touch the code.
- **Keep the contrast.** Dark code on a light background. Avoid putting it over a photo or a busy pattern, and avoid light-on-dark colour schemes unless you've tested them.

## Step 5: Get the size and placement right

Most flyer codes that fail are too small for the distance people scan them from. The Nielsen Norman Group's [QR code usability guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/qr-code-guidelines/) recommend at least 2 x 2 cm (0.8 inches), plus 1 cm of width for every 10 cm of scanning distance. In other words, a code scans well from about ten times its own width.

| Where the flyer is read | Typical distance | Minimum code width |
|---|---|---|
| Handout, table flyer, leaflet | 25 to 30 cm (arm's length) | 2.5 to 3 cm (about 1 inch) |
| A4 or Letter flyer on a noticeboard | About 1 metre | 10 cm (4 inches) |
| Poster in a window or on a wall | 2 metres or more | 20 cm (8 inches) or larger |

Go bigger if you can. Phone cameras struggle in dim light, behind glass and at an angle, and a bigger code makes up for all three.

Where to put it on the page:

- **Next to the call to action.** Put the code beside the line that tells people what to do ("Book your spot", "Order by Friday"), not in whatever corner was left over.
- **Away from folds, edges and cut lines.** A crease through a code can break it, and print shops trim a few millimetres off the edge.
- **With words that say what happens.** "Scan to book your spot" or "Scan for the full menu" tells people what they get. A bare square gives them no reason to pull out a phone.
- **With a typed link underneath.** Some people won't scan. A short, readable link, like `yourbrand.com/spring` or a branded short link, gives them another way in.

## Step 6: Test one copy before you print the rest

Print a single copy on the paper you'll use. Then:

1. Scan it with an iPhone and an Android phone, from the distance people will actually stand.
2. Try it in the light where the flyer will hang, including behind glass if it's going in a window.
3. Check that the page loads on mobile data, not just your Wi-Fi.
4. If you used a dynamic code, check the scan shows up in your dashboard.

Glossy paper reflects light into the camera. If scans are patchy on the test print, try matte.

## Step 7: Track which flyers work

Say you print 500 flyers, hand them out in five places, and a few people sign up. Which places worked? Without tracking you can't tell, so the next print run is a guess too.

Use **one code per place or batch you want to compare**, for example:

- Code 1: flyers at the café counter
- Code 2: the library noticeboard
- Code 3: door-to-door drop in one neighbourhood
- Code 4: handed out at the weekend market

Each code points to the same page, but each one counts its own scans. After two weeks you'll see that, say, the café brought 60 scans and the door drop brought 4, and you'll know where to put the next print run.

## After the event: repoint the code

Your flyers will keep getting scanned after the date has passed. They'll still be on noticeboards, in bags and on fridges. With a dynamic code, edit the destination once the event is over:

- Point it at photos or a short recap.
- Point it at the next date or a waitlist.
- Point it at a "join our mailing list" page so late scanners still become contacts.

It takes a minute, and people who find an old flyer land somewhere useful instead of on a page for an event that's over. Churches and charities that print every week can use the same code over and over; see [QR codes for churches and nonprofits](/solutions/churches-and-nonprofits).
